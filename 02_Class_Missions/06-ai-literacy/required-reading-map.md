# Required In-Class Reading Map — Grade 7

This map states exactly which **content** students read in each meeting. It uses chapter-and-idea anchors because page numbers and line breaks differ across print, ebook, and translated editions.

## Teacher Must Complete Before Each Meeting

1. Find the listed start and stop anchors in the class edition.
2. Select only the stated content, keeping the total within the listed word range.
3. Write the exact page range beside the meeting code.
4. If an anchor spans too many words in the class edition, keep the named case and the author's conclusion; omit background details named under **Do not require**.
5. Do not photocopy or post more text than the school's book access and copyright arrangements allow.

No full chapter is required. No part of this map is assigned before class or as routine homework.

## Meeting-by-Meeting Selections

### 21A — What Counts as Intelligence?

- **Required chapters:** Prologue + Chapter 1, “The Roots of Artificial Intelligence”
- **Chunk 1 — start/stop anchor:** In the Prologue, begin where Mitchell recounts Douglas Hofstadter's changed reaction to recent AI progress. Stop after the passage establishes why impressive performance raises questions about what the system actually understands.
- **Chunk 2 — start/stop anchor:** In Chapter 1, read the passage that treats intelligence as a many-meaning “suitcase” concept and distinguishes the scientific goal of understanding intelligence from the practical goal of building useful intelligent systems. Stop after both goals are clear.
- **Required load:** **300–400 words total**; two chunks, each no more than about 200 words
- **Required vocabulary:** intelligence, evidence, understanding, consciousness
- **Do not require:** the full Prologue; full researcher biographies; a complete history of the Dartmouth meeting; any passage not needed to explain the definition problem and the two AI goals
- **Teacher page range:** Prologue pp. ___–___; Chapter 1 pp. ___–___

### 21B — Is AI One Method?

- **Required chapter:** Chapter 1, “The Roots of Artificial Intelligence”
- **Start/stop anchor:** Begin with Mitchell's description of AI as a field containing competing methods rather than a single agreed approach. Continue through her contrast between symbolic approaches, which use explicit symbols/rules, and subsymbolic approaches, which learn distributed numerical patterns. Stop once machine learning and deep learning are located within that contrast.
- **Required load:** **300–400 words**, one continuous selection; insert a pause near the symbolic/subsymbolic transition
- **Required vocabulary:** symbolic, subsymbolic, machine learning, deep learning
- **Do not require:** a complete timeline of AI; every named school or researcher; technical details beyond the book's introductory contrast
- **Teacher page range:** pp. ___–___

### 21C — Learning Evidence and AI Expectations

- **Required chapters:** Chapter 2, “Neural Networks and the Ascent of Machine Learning” + Chapter 3, “AI Spring”
- **Chunk 1 — start/stop anchor:** In Chapter 2, read the digit-recognition example from the explanation of inputs and learned weights through the basic purpose of backpropagation: adjusting weights when the network's answer is wrong. Stop before extended mathematical or historical detail.
- **Chunk 2 — start/stop anchor:** In Chapter 3, read the passage describing the new “AI spring” and contrasting present narrow/task-specific systems with the broader idea of general intelligence. Stop after the gap between demonstrated success and larger expectation is clear.
- **Required load:** **350–450 words total**; two chunks, each no more than about 225 words
- **Required vocabulary:** neural network, weight, backpropagation, narrow AI
- **Do not require:** equations; the full neural-network training derivation; every milestone or company example; the full chapter history
- **Teacher page range:** Chapter 2 pp. ___–___; Chapter 3 pp. ___–___

### 22A — Labels and Scene Understanding

- **Required chapter:** Chapter 4, “Who, What, When, Where, and Why”
- **Start/stop anchor:** Read Mitchell's analysis of the soldier-and-dog reunion photograph. Begin where she asks what is visibly present in the image. Continue through the human inferences about who the figures are, what is happening, and why the moment matters. Stop after she contrasts those contextual inferences with recognizing or labeling visible objects.
- **Required load:** **300–400 words**, one continuous selection with a pause after the visible details
- **Required vocabulary:** classification, perception, context, inference
- **Do not require:** the entire chapter overview of computer vision; other image cases; technical architecture details
- **Teacher page range:** pp. ___–___

### 22B — What Does a Benchmark Show?

- **Required chapter:** Chapter 5, “ConvNets and ImageNet”
- **Start/stop anchor:** Begin with why the ImageNet dataset and competition were created. Continue through the AlexNet result in the 2012 competition and Mitchell's explanation of why that result changed the field. Stop after the caveat that a benchmark score—including a comparison described as “human-level”—shows performance on a defined test, not full human vision or scene understanding.
- **Required load:** **350–450 words**, one continuous selection; pause after the reported result
- **Required vocabulary:** dataset, benchmark, accuracy, generalization
- **Do not require:** convolution equations; every layer of AlexNet; all competition years and scores; researcher biographies not needed for the claim
- **Teacher page range:** pp. ___–___

### 22C — From Failure to Trust

- **Required chapters:** Chapter 6, “A Closer Look at Machines That Learn” + Chapter 7, “On Trustworthy and Ethical AI”
- **Chunk 1 — start/stop anchor:** In Chapter 6, read the adversarial-image example in which a small change that does not alter the image's meaning for a person causes a confident wrong classification. Begin with the original image/classification, continue through the altered result, and stop after Mitchell explains what this failure reveals about the features the system learned.
- **Chunk 2 — start/stop anchor:** In Chapter 7, read one facial-recognition case from the reported error or unequal performance through the discussion of who is affected and why accuracy alone is not enough for trust or deployment.
- **Required load:** **350–450 words total**; two chunks, each no more than about 225 words
- **Required vocabulary:** shortcut, adversarial, bias, trust
- **Do not require:** long-tail and other Chapter 6 failure cases; every ethical principle; current examples added from outside the book; a second facial-recognition case
- **Teacher page range:** Chapter 6 pp. ___–___; Chapter 7 pp. ___–___

### 23A — Rosie's Reward Loop

- **Required chapter:** Chapter 8, “Rewards for Robots”
- **Start/stop anchor:** Begin with Rosie's robot-dog soccer task and the goal the agent is given. Continue through the explanation of state, possible action, reward, and learning from repeated attempts. Stop after the discussion of exploration versus exploitation and the possibility that an agent can learn an accidental or “superstitious” behavior that earns reward without understanding the human purpose.
- **Required load:** **350–450 words**, one continuous selection divided into two reading chunks
- **Required vocabulary:** agent, state, action, reward
- **Do not require:** reinforcement-learning equations; a full Q-learning algorithm; all robotics examples; implementation details not needed to trace the loop
- **Teacher page range:** pp. ___–___

### 23B — Game Mastery and Transfer

- **Required chapters:** Chapter 9, “Game On” + Chapter 10, “Beyond Games”
- **Chunk 1 — start/stop anchor:** In Chapter 9, read the AlphaGo achievement from the description of its Go task/training through its demonstrated match result and Mitchell's explanation of why the result was technically important.
- **Chunk 2 — start/stop anchor:** In Chapter 10, read the passage that tests the stronger interpretation of that result. Begin where Mitchell asks what happens outside the trained game or after the task changes, and stop after the limit on transfer or broad generalization is stated.
- **Required load:** **300–400 words total**; two chunks of about 150–200 words
- **Required vocabulary:** transfer, specialized, generalize, environment
- **Do not require:** the Atari case; complete game histories; search-tree or hardware details; every example in Chapter 10
- **Teacher page range:** Chapter 9 pp. ___–___; Chapter 10 pp. ___–___

### 23C — Words, Context, and Common Sense

- **Required chapter:** Chapter 11, “Words and Company”
- **Chunk 1 — start/stop anchor:** Read the burned-hamburger story from the short scenario through Mitchell's explanation of the unstated background knowledge a reader supplies to understand what happened.
- **Chunk 2 — start/stop anchor:** Read the later introductory passage explaining how word representations learn meaning-like patterns from the words that occur nearby. Stop after the text identifies both what these patterns make possible and one limit or bias they can inherit.
- **Required load:** **300–400 words total**; two chunks of about 150–200 words
- **Required vocabulary:** common sense, context, word vector, bias
- **Do not require:** all word-vector examples; vector mathematics; the full history of language processing; outside chatbot examples
- **Teacher page range:** Chunk 1 pp. ___–___; Chunk 2 pp. ___–___

### 24A — Correct Language and Comprehension

- **Required chapters:** Chapter 12, “Translation” + Chapter 13, “Ask Me Anything”
- **Chunk 1 — start/stop anchor:** In Chapter 12, read the high-level explanation of an encoder-decoder translation system: how an input sentence is represented and an output sentence is produced. Stop after Mitchell identifies a context or meaning limitation that can remain even when the translation sounds good.
- **Chunk 2 — start/stop anchor:** In Chapter 13, read the SQuAD passage from the benchmark task and reported success through Mitchell's explanation of why extracting answers to those questions is not the same as broad reading comprehension.
- **Required load:** **350–450 words total**; two chunks, each no more than about 225 words
- **Required vocabulary:** encoder, decoder, benchmark, comprehension
- **Do not require:** the Watson case; architecture diagrams or equations; all translation history; current language-model examples outside the book
- **Teacher page range:** Chapter 12 pp. ___–___; Chapter 13 pp. ___–___

### 24B — Meaning, Core Knowledge, and Analogy

- **Required chapters:** Chapter 14, “On Understanding” + Chapter 15, “Knowledge, Abstraction, and Analogy”
- **Chunk 1 — start/stop anchor:** In Chapter 14, read the passage introducing the “barrier of meaning” and explaining why core knowledge, background knowledge, or mental simulation matters to human understanding. Stop after one concrete example makes the claim clear.
- **Chunk 2 — start/stop anchor:** In Chapter 15, read one Bongard problem from its two groups of visual examples and classification challenge through Mitchell's explanation of how abstraction and relations—not surface matching alone—support a solution.
- **Required load:** **350–450 words total**; two chunks, each no more than about 225 words
- **Required vocabulary:** meaning, core knowledge, abstraction, analogy
- **Do not require:** Copycat; the full Cyc discussion; every proposed ingredient of understanding; program details
- **Teacher page range:** Chapter 14 pp. ___–___; Chapter 15 pp. ___–___

### 24C — A Thinking Human's Judgement

- **Required chapter:** Chapter 16, “Questions, Answers, and Speculations”
- **Start/stop anchor:** Read the concluding passage in which Mitchell weighs evidence about progress toward general or human-like intelligence, keeps uncertainty about forecasts, and distinguishes concrete risks or consequences from dramatic predictions. Stop after the book's final cautious guidance about evaluating AI claims is clear.
- **Required load:** **300–400 words**, one continuous selection with a midpoint pause
- **Required vocabulary:** general intelligence, uncertainty, evidence, risk
- **Also used in class:** students' own book-based Learning Logs from at least three earlier meetings; these are prior evidence records, not a new content source
- **Do not require:** rereading all of Chapter 16; rereading earlier chapters; an outside “latest AI” article; a take-home essay
- **Teacher page range:** pp. ___–___

## If the Class Edition Does Not Match an Anchor

Use the table of contents to locate the named case or concept. Keep the **case + Mitchell's interpretation + evidence boundary**. Do not substitute an online summary. If a translation expands the word count, stop at the same conceptual endpoint even if the page count is longer.
