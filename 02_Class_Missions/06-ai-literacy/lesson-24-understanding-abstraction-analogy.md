# Lesson 24 — Language, Understanding, Analogy, and Synthesis

**Students:** Grade 7  
**Delivery:** three 45-minute class meetings: **24A, 24B, and 24C**  
**Only required content source:** Melanie Mitchell, *Artificial Intelligence: A Guide for Thinking Humans*  
**Student preparation:** none  
**Regular homework:** none  
**Flow:** [Grade 7 AI Literacy 45-Minute Classroom Flow](classroom-flow-45-min.md)  
**Reading locations:** [Required In-Class Reading Map](required-reading-map.md)

## Lesson Purpose

Students examine successful translation and question answering without equating correctness with full comprehension. They then explore Mitchell's arguments about meaning, core knowledge, abstraction, and analogy before building a cautious whole-unit judgement from book evidence.

## Required Mastery

By the end of Meeting 24C, students can:

1. state the bounded task demonstrated by a translation or question-answering result;
2. explain one context or comprehension limit;
3. distinguish surface similarity from a relational analogy;
4. describe why background/core knowledge matters to Mitchell's account of understanding;
5. support an overall AI judgement with at least three book cases and clear evidence boundaries;
6. state uncertainty and identify evidence that could change the judgement.

## Teacher Setup for All Three Meetings

- Locate and record the exact pages using the [Required In-Class Reading Map](required-reading-map.md).
- Keep each meeting within the stated **300–450-word** limit.
- Display only the four listed vocabulary words.
- Prepare the half-page organizer named for each meeting.
- Before 24C, ask students to bring or open their own Learning Logs from at least three earlier meetings; this is not homework and introduces no new source.
- Do not add current AI news, a product page, a video, an online summary, or a live chatbot.

## Meeting 24A — Correct Language and Comprehension

**Essential question:** Is a correct language answer evidence of understanding?

**Required in-class reading — 350–450 words:** two chunks: (1) Chapter 12's high-level encoder-decoder translation explanation through one remaining context/meaning limitation; (2) Chapter 13's SQuAD passage, from the defined benchmark and reported success through Mitchell's explanation of why extracting those answers is not broad reading comprehension. Use the exact anchors and exclusions in [24A of the reading map](required-reading-map.md#24a--correct-language-and-comprehension).

| Time | Classroom-flow block | Required student action |
|---:|---|---|
| 0–3 | **Skill Warm-up** | Complete: “A correct answer is evidence that ___; it may not show ___.” |
| 3–17 | **Guided Practice** | Read both chunks. For translation, mark input, representation/process, output, and context limit. For question answering, mark task, benchmark result, and comprehension boundary. Paraphrase each chunk once. |
| 17–22 | **Talk Robin 1** | Partners use: “The system succeeds at ___ under ___ conditions. Mitchell's example shows a limit when ___.” Cite one page from each chapter. |
| 22–28 | **Core Pattern** | Pairs show one capability and one boundary. Teacher clarifies only shared confusion about encoder, decoder, benchmark, or comprehension. |
| 28–38 | **Guided Practice** | Complete a two-row **Language Capability Boundary Table**: one row for translation and one for question answering. Teacher models the defined task column; students add evidence, successful capability, missing context/knowledge, and unsupported stronger claim. |
| 38–43 | **Independent Rebuild** | Independently complete the four Learning Log lines for Meeting 24A. The boundary line must explain why correctness is insufficient evidence of broad comprehension. |
| 43–45 | **Talk Robin 2 + Evidence** | Submit the Capability Table and Learning Log entry. |

### Language Capability Boundary Table

| Case | Defined task | Evidence of success | Missing context/knowledge shown by the case | Stronger claim not proved |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Translation | short phrase | book detail + page | one limit | one claim |
| Question answering | short phrase | book detail + page | one limit | one claim |

**Success evidence:** The student names a real language capability without treating correct output as automatic proof of broad comprehension.

## Meeting 24B — Meaning, Core Knowledge, and Analogy

**Essential question:** What may human-like understanding require beyond successful pattern matching?

**Required in-class reading — 350–450 words:** two chunks: (1) the Chapter 14 “barrier of meaning” passage through one example showing the role of core/background knowledge or mental simulation; (2) one Chapter 15 Bongard problem, from the two groups of visual examples and classification challenge through Mitchell's explanation of how abstraction and relational analogy support a solution. Use the exact anchors and exclusions in [24B of the reading map](required-reading-map.md#24b--meaning-core-knowledge-and-analogy).

| Time | Classroom-flow block | Required student action |
|---:|---|---|
| 0–3 | **Skill Warm-up** | Compare two simple pairs. Choose which pair shares the same relationship rather than only a similar appearance, and state the relationship. |
| 3–17 | **Guided Practice** | Read both chunks. In Chapter 14 mark claim, background/core knowledge, and example. In Chapter 15 mark source, target, shared relation, ignored surface detail, and failure point. Paraphrase each chunk once. |
| 17–22 | **Talk Robin 1** | Partners use: “These cases look different, but both contain the relation ___.” Then state one place the analogy would stop working. Cite a page. |
| 22–28 | **Core Pattern** | Pairs show one relational mapping and one boundary. Teacher clarifies only shared confusion about meaning, core knowledge, abstraction, or analogy. |
| 28–38 | **Guided Practice** | Build a **Relation Map + Failure Boundary** from the selected Chapter 15 case, then add one note from Chapter 14 explaining what background knowledge makes the mapping meaningful. Teacher models one mapped relation. |
| 38–43 | **Independent Rebuild** | Independently complete the four Learning Log lines for Meeting 24B. The evidence line names the selected case; the boundary line distinguishes relation mapping from surface matching. |
| 43–45 | **Talk Robin 2 + Evidence** | Submit the Relation Map and Learning Log entry. |

### Relation Map + Failure Boundary

| Source element/relation | Target element/relation | Surface detail ignored | Background/core knowledge used | Where the analogy breaks |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| one book element | mapped element | short phrase | short phrase | one boundary |
| one relation | mapped relation | short phrase | short phrase | one boundary |

**Success evidence:** The student maps at least one relation and names a real boundary instead of describing analogy as appearance matching.

## Meeting 24C — A Thinking Human's Judgement

**Essential question:** Based on this book's evidence, how should a careful thinker judge claims about AI?

**Required in-class reading — 300–400 words:** the Chapter 16 concluding passage that weighs evidence about progress toward general/human-like intelligence, preserves uncertainty about forecasts, and separates concrete risks or consequences from dramatic predictions. Students also consult at least three of their own earlier book-based Learning Logs. Use the exact anchor and exclusions in [24C of the reading map](required-reading-map.md#24c--a-thinking-humans-judgement).

| Time | Classroom-flow block | Required student action |
|---:|---|---|
| 0–3 | **Skill Warm-up** | Place separate marks—not one overall score—on four 1–4 scales: task performance, transfer, understanding, and consciousness. |
| 3–17 | **Guided Practice** | Read the Chapter 16 selection. Mark O (overall claim), E (evidence), U (uncertainty), and R (risk). At the midpoint, paraphrase; at the end, write Mitchell's cautious conclusion in one sentence. |
| 17–22 | **Talk Robin 1** | Partners compare Chapter 16 with three earlier Learning Logs. Each selects three cases and states whether each supports, limits, or complicates an overall judgement. |
| 22–28 | **Core Pattern** | Pairs show one conclusion, one counterpoint, and one uncertainty. Teacher clarifies only a shared failure to separate capability, interpretation, forecast, and risk. |
| 28–38 | **Guided Practice** | Independently complete a short **Thinking Human's AI Brief**: one bounded overall claim, three one-line book cases with page/log references, one fair counterargument, and one specific change condition. Sentence frames are allowed. |
| 38–43 | **Independent Rebuild** | Independently complete the four Learning Log lines for Meeting 24C. The final line states the most important unresolved question or evidence that could change the student's view. |
| 43–45 | **Talk Robin 2 + Evidence** | Submit the Thinking Human's AI Brief and Learning Log entry. |

### Thinking Human's AI Brief

**Overall judgement frame:** “The book's evidence supports the claim that AI can ___, but it does not yet establish ___.”

| Book case | Demonstrated capability | Evidence boundary | Page or earlier log |
|---|---|---|---|
| case 1 | short phrase | short phrase | reference |
| case 2 | short phrase | short phrase | reference |
| case 3 | short phrase | short phrase | reference |

- **Fair counterargument:** “Someone could reasonably argue ___ because ___.”
- **Change condition:** “I would revise my judgement if evidence showed ___.”

**Success evidence:** The student supports a precise judgement with three book cases, represents a counterargument fairly, and preserves uncertainty.

## Finish-Up Rule

All required reading and the brief happen during class. If time is lost, only unfinished Learning Log lines may be completed later, normally within five minutes. The Brief is not a take-home essay.
