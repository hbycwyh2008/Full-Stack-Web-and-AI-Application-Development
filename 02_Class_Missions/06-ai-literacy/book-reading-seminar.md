# AI Literacy Book Seminar: Eight Meetings

**Course:** Full Stack Web and AI Application Development  
**Core text:** Melanie Mitchell, *Artificial Intelligence: A Guide for Thinking Humans* (2019)  
**Format:** Eight 45-minute meetings; student-led expert reading and teaching  
**Guiding question:** What can AI do, where are its limits, and how should we use it responsibly?

## Learning outcomes

By the end of the seminar, students can:

- Explain how AI approaches and machine learning have changed over time.
- Use a book example and page reference to support a claim about AI.
- Distinguish a system's performance on a task from broad understanding or general intelligence.
- Identify ethical trade-offs, failure modes, and the limits of benchmarks.
- Explain the basics of language representation, translation, and question answering.
- Use generative AI as a tool while checking outputs, protecting private information, and taking responsibility for final work.
- Combine multiple groups' findings into one coherent class presentation.

## Eight-meeting sequence

Groups read their assigned chapter or excerpt and prepare before the meeting. The teacher supplies brief excerpts, vocabulary support, and page references as needed. The complete chapter does not need to be read aloud in class.

| Meeting | Reading and expert team(s) | Core question / evidence |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Chapter 1: AI's roots, history, and approaches | What different ideas of intelligence shaped AI? Compare rule-based and learning approaches using the author's examples. |
| 2 | Chapter 2: neural networks and the rise of machine learning | What changed as machine learning grew, and what do AI, ML, and deep learning mean in context? |
| 3 | Chapter 7, Team A: “The Great AI Trade-Off” and “The Ethics of Face Recognition” | Who benefits and who bears the costs? What does the face-recognition case show about data, bias, privacy, and deployment? |
| 4 | Chapter 7, Team B: “Regulating AI” and “Moral AI” | Who should set limits and bear responsibility? What can technical rules address, and what remains a human decision? |
| 5 | Chapters 11–12: “Words” and “Translation as Encoding and Decoding” | How are words represented and translated? What does successful language processing reveal—and fail to reveal—about meaning? |
| 6 | Chapter 13: “Ask Me Anything” | What did question-answering systems such as Watson do well? What can a benchmark result tell us, and what can it not tell us? |
| 7 | Chapters 14–15: “On Understanding”; “Knowledge, Abstraction, and Analogy in AI” | How does the author distinguish fluent performance from understanding? Why do knowledge, abstraction, and analogy matter? |
| 8 | Chapter 16: “Questions, Answers, and Speculations”; final class synthesis deck | Which questions remain open? What is the class's evidence-based answer to the seminar's guiding question? |

**Chapter 7 split:** The two teams cover different subsections during separate meetings. Each team should make its own argument and connect it to the other team's evidence during Q&A.

**Contemporary AI note:** The book was published in 2019. Chapter 13 is about question-answering systems and Watson, not ChatGPT. Any activity about current generative AI or prompting is a clearly labeled teacher-created supplement.

## Group expert presentation

Each group prepares a short, approximately five-slide deck:

1. **Question:** What problem or question is this section addressing?
2. **Claim:** What is Mitchell's main point? Put it in your own words.
3. **Book evidence:** Use one or two examples and cite printed page numbers. If page numbers vary by edition, include the chapter and section heading as well.
4. **Capability and limit:** What can the described AI do? What does the example not establish?
5. **Connection and discussion:** Connect it to a present-day example or Full Stack project; pose one question for classmates.

Present for about 5–6 minutes, then take questions. Avoid slide-by-slide plot summaries and unsupported claims. Keep quotations short; explain the evidence in students' own words.

### Audience note-catcher

For each presentation, listeners record:

- **Claim:** What is the group's main point?
- **Evidence:** Which book example supports it?
- **Question:** What remains uncertain, limited, or debatable?

This gives students a reason to listen and provides source material for the final synthesis deck.

## 45-minute meeting structure

A workable default for a day with two presenting groups:

| Time | Activity |
|---|---|
| 0–4 min | Opening question and purpose |
| 4–16 min | Two expert talks, about 5–6 minutes each |
| 16–23 min | Class questions and discussion |
| 23–35 min | Short application, evidence comparison, or case activity |
| 35–41 min | Connect to contemporary AI or the Weather App / Full Stack project |
| 41–45 min | Exit ticket: claim, evidence, limitation, or remaining question |

Adjust the number of speakers, reading support, and activity to class size and English proficiency. Preserve student thinking and evidence checking even when a presentation takes longer.

## Final integrated class presentation

The final deck is a class synthesis, not a concatenation of group decks. Build it using the audience note-catchers and group source slides, then edit for one shared through-line. The Chapter 16 team leads the open-question section; all teams contribute one claim and one cited piece of evidence.

Suggested narrative:

1. How ideas about AI developed: origins, approaches, learning, and neural networks.
2. What AI systems can do and how capability claims should be evaluated.
3. Trade-offs: trust, bias, privacy, regulation, and responsibility.
4. What language, translation, and question answering show about performance and meaning.
5. Understanding, knowledge, abstraction, analogy, and unresolved limits.
6. What questions remain, including the author's speculations.
7. A clearly labeled contemporary supplement: today's generative AI and the Define → Plan → Try → Iterate workflow.
8. What responsible use looks like in this Full Stack course and its projects.

Use a shared title and conclusion, consistent terminology, readable evidence citations, and clear transitions between sections. Remove duplicated explanations. The final audience should be able to answer the guiding question after seeing the deck.

## Prompting as a contemporary problem-solving supplement

Teach prompting as an iterative work process, not a bag of magic phrases. Students can use the following scaffold:

- **Define:** “I am trying to [goal] for [user/context]. I have [relevant information]. Success means [observable criteria]. Ask me for missing information before proposing a solution.”
- **Plan:** “Suggest a short plan and state assumptions or risks. Do not write the full solution yet.”
- **Try:** “Help me implement or test only step [N]. Explain what I should observe. I will run it and report the actual result.”
- **Iterate:** “Here is what actually happened: [output/error/evidence]. Compare it with [success criteria]. Suggest one small change and a way to retest.”

**Weather App example:** Define the display requirement and data source; plan how to inspect the API response; try displaying one field; run the app and compare actual output; iterate based on a real error or mismatch. Do not paste API keys or personal data into a public model.

Students must read, run, test, and explain their final work. They should check factual claims against reliable sources and disclose AI assistance as required by course policy.

## Assessment

Use evidence of learning rather than slide polish alone:

- Book claim accurately represented and supported with page/section references.
- Presentation distinguishes capability from limitation.
- Each listener records claims, evidence, and questions.
- Final deck integrates rather than duplicates group work.
- Exit ticket names one supported conclusion and one remaining limitation or question.
- Prompting activity shows the student's own test, verification, and revision.

A simple group rubric may use four criteria, each scored 0–2: **accuracy and evidence, explanation of capability/limits, teaching and discussion, contribution to the integrated deck**. Score each student's individual participation and final reflection separately from the group's slide design.

## Teacher preparation

- Assign one primary expert group per section; split Chapter 7 as shown.
- Provide an accessible excerpt, key terms, and edition/page guidance where needed.
- Collect group slides before class so book citations and claims can be checked.
- Use a shared slide template or note-catcher, but allow students to choose examples and organize the explanation.
- Maintain a visible source list for book claims and contemporary supplements.
- If there is not enough time for eight meetings, use the [Six-Theme Foundation Path](foundation-path.md) as an alternative; do not require both routes cumulatively.
