# AI Literacy — Six-Theme Foundation Path

**Audience:** Full Stack students who need more support with programming and reading.
**Core text:** Melanie Mitchell, *Artificial Intelligence: A Guide for Thinking Humans*. [Author's book page](https://melaniemitchell.me/aibook/).
**Planning default:** six 45-minute meetings in the L21–L24 unit, with extra practice if needed. No required pre-class reading or regular reading homework. This is not the ASC eight-session plan or the twelve-meeting extension route.

## Core Standard

Students can **recognize AI, use it purposefully, check its outputs and take responsibility**. They explain in plain language, use a concrete example and show what they checked. Technical vocabulary alone is not mastery.

The book is the conceptual spine. Short teacher-created cases support transfer; present-day generative-AI use is an explicitly identified practical supplement, not content attributed to Mitchell's original text. No second full AI video course is required.

## Reading Access

- The teacher chooses a short, complete idea from a legally accessed edition and records pages before class. Chapter references below are topic anchors, not a demand to read a chapter.
- Begin with approximately 150–250 English words per meeting, in small chunks, or an equivalent short selection from an authorized translated edition. Adjust to demonstrated comprehension.
- Read in class; allow read-aloud, a few vocabulary glosses, pictures and sentence frames. Pause for students to paraphrase.
- Assess the idea and evidence, not English fluency. Do not upload substantial book text to the public repository or to AI services without appropriate rights.
- If learners need longer, add a meeting; do not move unfinished chapter reading into compulsory homework.

## Six Meetings

| Code / lesson anchor | Must know | Reading anchor / input | Classroom activity | Individual evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| F1 / L21 | AI is a field with different methods; some programs use explicit rules while others learn from examples | Ch1: AI goals and approaches; brief historical context only | Compare a calculator/rule program, spam classifier and chatbot; avoid claiming every AI is machine learning | Explain one comparison in your own words |
| F2 / L22 | Examples/data → learned patterns → predictions on new inputs; training differs from use | Ch2: digit-recognition/learning example, without weight-update derivation | Draw a training/use diagram; optionally try a teacher-prepared small classification demonstration after reading | Label the input, learning stage and output; explain one new-example prediction |
| F3 / L23 | Recognize classification, numerical prediction and generation; state input/output | Short chosen vision/language case from Ch4 or Ch11–12; teacher adds clearly labeled task examples | Sort task cards and justify choices; allow multiple valid descriptions when a system combines tasks | Classify three new cases and specify input/output |
| F4 / L22 | Data gaps, bias and new contexts can cause failure; good performance is not proof of human-like understanding | One Ch6–7 failure/trust case | Compare success and failure; separate observation from explanation | State what happened, a plausible cause, and one check before trusting it |
| F5 / L23 | Give goal/context/requirements; iterate and verify facts or code | Brief Ch12–13 capability/meaning discussion plus explicitly separate teacher-created generative-AI exercise | Compare two prompts on the same task; verify the result using a reliable reference or execution, not just another AI answer | Prompt → output → check → correction record |
| F6 / L24 | Protect personal information; disclose AI assistance; consider bias; own the final work | Short Ch7 or Ch16 responsibility/risk passage plus the course AI policy | Judge classroom use scenarios and explain a responsible next action | Scenario response, disclosure and final four-question check |

F-codes are meeting identifiers within the existing unit, not a renumbering of the repository's 00–32 lessons. Revisit a previous idea where useful. History is a short context, not an extra memorization-heavy unit.

## Keep the 45-Minute Classroom Flow

| Time | Existing block | Foundation activity |
|---|---|---|
| 0–5 | Skill Warm-up | Picture, familiar example or short prediction |
| 5–9 | Talk Robin 1 | Students share one observation and one question |
| 9–14 | Entry Check | Individual diagnosis of the day's key distinction |
| 14–19 | Core Pattern | Short teacher explanation and vocabulary support |
| 19–30 | Guided Practice | Read the selected passage, paraphrase, then work on a case |
| 30–40 | Independent Rebuild | A new example/transfer task without AI assistance |
| 40–45 | Talk Robin 2 + Evidence | Explain, note uncertainty and submit the day's evidence |

Do not silently replace this schedule with a mandatory pre-class reading or a 15-minute book lecture in every programming lesson. Responsible-use habits continue during development. Before any AI-assisted coding activity, teach/revisit F5–F6 essentials and the [student AI policy](../../01_Student_Start/04_AI_Use_Policy_For_Students.md).

For a live AI activity, use school-approved access and synthetic data. If access is unavailable, use a teacher-prepared fictional response with an intentional checkable error; label it as such. The learning outcome is checking and judgment, not account creation.

## What to Defer

No required backpropagation equations, internal CNN/RNN architecture, reinforcement-learning algorithms, AlphaGo implementation, vector mathematics, or extended philosophical debate about abstraction/analogy. Interested students can later use the [twelve-meeting reading extension](required-reading-map.md).

## Assessment

Use short checks after each theme and [AI Literacy Checklist](../../04_Assessment/AI_Literacy_Checklist.md). Ask students to answer these about a new example:

1. What is AI doing here? What are the input and output?
2. Where could it fail, and why?
3. How would you check this particular result?
4. What privacy, disclosure or responsibility issue matters?

Accept a drawing and oral explanation. Require a specific verification action (checking a source, testing code, comparing against known data) rather than “ask AI again.” Collect the original, result, check and revision when AI is used. Reassess missing skills with a new small example.

## Optional Depth

The existing L21–L24 long-form lesson files, reading map and logs remain an extension. They are not additional compulsory work for this foundation route. AI literacy is assessed even when the final web project contains no AI feature.
